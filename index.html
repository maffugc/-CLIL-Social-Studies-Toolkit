What is CLIL?

Start here before planning the activities.

CONTENT + LANGUAGE INTEGRATED LEARNING

Two goals, one lesson.

CLIL means students learn subject content while using a foreign language as a tool for learning and communication.

Remember: Social Studies remains the subject. English is the vehicle.
SOCIAL

CLIL is not…

It is not simply teaching Social Studies with difficult English.

❌ A grammar lesson with Social Studies words.
❌ Translating the whole subject into English.
❌ Giving long texts without support.
❌ “English only” at all costs.

Instead…

Choose meaningful content, make the language accessible, support thinking, and give students a reason to communicate.

The 4Cs Framework

Four lenses for designing a meaningful CLIL lesson.

📚

Content

What Social Studies knowledge or concept should students learn?

Example: Causes of migration.
💬

Communication

What language will students need to access and communicate the content?

Example: “People migrate because…”
🧠

Cognition

What thinking will students do with the content?

Example: classify → compare → explain.
🌍

Culture

What perspectives, communities or real-world connections can students explore?

Example: different migration experiences.

The 4Cs working together

Content: students learn why people migrate. Communication: they use “because” and key vocabulary. Cognition: they classify reasons and justify a choice. Culture: they consider different experiences and perspectives.

CLIL concepts — one concept at a time

These concepts explain what happens between the 4Cs and the actual classroom task. Each one includes a Social Studies example and a practical teacher move.

📚
01 · LANGUAGE OF LEARNING

The language of the topic

The words and expressions students need to understand the subject itself. This is the language that belongs to Social Studies content.

Social Studies example — Migration:
migrate · border · population · reason · safety · employment

In class: Students match each word to an image and then use “People migrate because…”
Teacher move: Choose only 5–8 words that are essential for the task. Do not pre-teach every word in the text.
💬
02 · LANGUAGE FOR LEARNING

The language of participation

The language students need to interact with classmates and complete the learning task: asking, agreeing, comparing, clarifying and explaining.

Social Studies example:
“What do you think?” · “I agree.” · “Can you explain?” · “Both are…” · “Unlike…”

In class: Pairs compare two migration stories and use one comparison frame.
Teacher move: Put useful frames where students can see them while they speak. Reuse them across activities.
✨
03 · LANGUAGE THROUGH LEARNING

Language that emerges

Language that students discover, need or develop while they are thinking about the content. You cannot always predict it beforehand.

Social Studies example:
During a comparison, students need “similar”, “different”, “both” or “unlike” even if these were not the original vocabulary target.

In class: The teacher adds “similar” to the board when students need it.
Teacher move: Listen for useful language that emerges and capture it without stopping the task for a long grammar explanation.
🪜
04 · SCAFFOLDING

Support access — not easier thinking

Scaffolding gives students temporary support so they can complete a task they could not yet complete independently.

Social Studies example:
A complex idea about migration is supported with a map + photo + 6-word bank + sentence frame + teacher model.

Scaffold ladder: visual → word bank → model → frame → independent response.
Teacher move: Ask “What is blocking access?” Then support that barrier instead of simplifying the Social Studies thinking.
🧠
05 · COGNITION · LOTS → HOTS

From noticing to reasoning

CLIL should develop thinking as well as language. Start with accessible cognitive actions and move toward more complex reasoning.

Same topic — different cognitive demand:
LOTS: identify countries on a map → classify migration reasons.
HOTS: compare reasons → explain which factor matters in a scenario → create a solution.
Teacher move: Keep the English accessible while increasing the quality of the thinking task.
🔄
06 · INPUT → OUTPUT

Understand, process, communicate

Students need accessible input before they can communicate meaningfully. Input becomes useful when students do something with it.

Social Studies example:
Map + short paragraph + image → students identify 3 facts → classify reasons → explain one conclusion to a partner.

Not: watch a video and answer “Did you understand?”
Teacher move: Give every input source one clear purpose: find, choose, sequence, compare or explain.

How to plan a CLIL lesson

A simple design routine before you open PowerPoint or Canva.

01 · CONTENT

Choose the learning

Write the Social Studies goal first.

Ask: What should students understand?
02 · LANGUAGE

Select 5–8 words

Add content vocabulary + action language + functional language.

Ask: What must they say/do?
03 · SCAFFOLD

Add access

Choose a visual, organizer, model, word bank or sentence frame.

Ask: What will make the content accessible?
04 · TASK

Make them think

Choose one meaningful action: classify, compare, sequence, explain or create.

Ask: What will students do with the content?

Your four-part class structure

CLIL fits inside the structure you already use.

01 · WARM-UP

Activate

Image, question, movement or familiar idea.

📷 “What do you notice?”
02 · PRESENTATION / INTRODUCTION

Give access

Essential content + 5–8 words + visual + model.

🗺️ Map + 3 key words.
03 · PRACTICE / DEVELOPMENT

Think + communicate

Pairs/groups classify, compare, sequence, identify or explain.

🧩 Sort causes into categories.
04 · CLOSURE / METACOGNITION

Reflect

Check content learning and the support students used.

💭 “One thing I can explain…”
CLIL flow: Activate → Access → Think → Communicate → Reflect

A complete model lesson

See all the pieces working together in one A2 Social Studies class.

MODEL CLIL CLASS · A2

Why do people migrate?

A complete example showing content, language, cognition, culture, scaffolding and interaction.

ContentCommunicationCognitionCultureVisualsNo-tech option

Can-do

Students can identify common reasons for migration and explain one reason using a supported sentence frame.

WARM-UP · 10 MIN

Look at the picture

Show a photo of a family travelling with luggage. Ask: “What do you see?” “Where could they be going?”

Teacher language: Look. What do you notice? Work with a partner. Choose one idea.
Student support: “I see…” · “Maybe they are…”
PRESENTATION / INTRODUCTION · 20 MIN

Build the concept

Use a simple map and introduce five words: migrate, border, reason, work, safety. Give a short explanation of migration with visuals.

Teacher language: Listen. This word means… Look at the map. Repeat. Which word matches the picture?
Student support: word bank + image cards + “People migrate because…”
PRACTICE / DEVELOPMENT · 35 MIN

Classify the reasons

Pairs receive cards: work, family, education, safety, weather. They classify them into categories and choose one important reason.

Teacher language: Match. Put the cards together. Compare with another pair. Why?
Student support: “I think ___ is a reason because ___.”
COMMUNICATIVE TASK · 20 MIN

Mini Migration Conference

Pairs choose one reason and give a 30-second explanation using the map or a card as visual support.

Teacher language: Rehearse first. Then share. One reason. One example.
Student support: “Our reason is ___. People migrate because ___. For example, ___.”
CLOSURE / METACOGNITION · 10 MIN

Exit reflection

Students complete: “Today I learned…” “One new word is…” “One thing I can explain is…”

Check: Did students understand the Social Studies idea? Which scaffold helped most?

Teacher & student language

Keep the English short, predictable and reusable.

🗣️

Teacher English

Short instructions reduce language load.

Look.Match.Choose.Compare.Work with a partner.Explain.
💬

Student English

Reuse the same frames across activities.

I think…Because…I agree.Both…Unlike…One reason is…
🔄

Strategic Spanish

Briefly clarify a concept when language would block access, then return to English.

The goal is meaningful access to Social Studies — not “English only” at all costs.

Three language buckets

CONTENT WORDS

government · border · population · rights · election

ACTION WORDS

identify · match · classify · compare · order · explain

FUNCTIONAL LANGUAGE

I think… · I agree… · because… · both… · unlike…
